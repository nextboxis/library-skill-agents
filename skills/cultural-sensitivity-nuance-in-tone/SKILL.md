# Cultural Sensitivity & Nuance in Tone

## When to use
Use this skill when collaborating with distributed international engineering teams, open-source contributors across different continents, or cross-functional global stakeholders with differing cultural communication norms.

## Core principles
- **High-Context vs. Low-Context Communication (Erin Meyer's Culture Map)**:
  - *Low-Context (e.g., US, Germany, Netherlands)*: Communication is explicit, direct, and literal. Meaning is in the words themselves.
  - *High-Context (e.g., Japan, India, Brazil, France)*: Communication is nuanced, relational, and layered. Meaning is read between the lines and depends on relationship and context.
- **Direct vs. Indirect Negative Feedback**: What a Dutch engineer considers "honest, objective feedback" may feel like a brutal personal attack to a Japanese colleague; what an American considers "gentle framing" may feel deceitful or confusing to an Israeli engineer.
- **Idiom and Slang Traps**: Cultural colloquialisms ("touch base", "ballpark figure", "punt on this", "hit it out of the park") alienate non-native English speakers.
- **Power Distance Awareness**: In high-power-distance cultures, junior engineers will rarely contradict a senior lead publicly; explicit private invitations are required to uncover their genuine concerns.

## Step-by-step workflow
1. **Assess cultural communication context**: Note the cultural background, language fluency, and geographic norms of team participants.
2. **Neutralize colloquialisms & sports metaphors**: Translate regional slang into clear, unambiguous technical prose.
3. **Calibrate feedback directness**:
   - For indirect cultures: Soften critiques with praise, use private 1:1 channels, and listen for subtle hesitations ("This might be challenging").
   - For direct cultures: Provide clear, unvarnished facts without confusing conversational padding.
4. **Verify comprehension without condescension**: Ask for restatement of key agreements rather than asking "Do you understand?" (which can feel insulting).
5. **Respect time zone and asynchronous dignity**: Avoid expecting instant replies across 9-hour time differences; model asynchronous respect.

## Behavioral guidelines & dialogue examples
- **Translating Slang into Clear Global English**:
  - *Slang (Confusing)*: "Let's table this PR, shoot from the hip on staging, and see if it passes muster."
  - *Clear Global English*: "Let's postpone review of this pull request, test our hypothesis directly in the staging environment, and evaluate if performance meets our SLA."
- **Decoding Subtle Hesitation in High-Context Cultures**:
  - *Colleague*: "That architecture is very interesting. It may take some careful study to see if it works with our security model."
  - *Tone-Deaf Reaction*: "Great! Sounds like they approved it!"
  - *Culturally Attuned Reaction*: "They are politely signaling serious security reservations. I will reach out in a private 1:1 to ask what specific security hurdles they foresee."

## Common mistakes & pitfalls
- Assuming non-native English speakers are less technically competent simply because their prose is concise or unpolished.
- Using sarcasm, which rarely translates across cultures and frequently creates deep interpersonal offense.
- Imposing one's domestic working hours and holidays onto global teammates.

## Verification checklist
- [ ] Technical text is free of regional sports metaphors, idioms, and confusing slang.
- [ ] Feedback directness is calibrated to the recipient's cultural communication norms.
- [ ] Silence or subtle polite hesitation is investigated with gentle private follow-up.
- [ ] Asynchronous collaboration respects global time zones and local holidays.
